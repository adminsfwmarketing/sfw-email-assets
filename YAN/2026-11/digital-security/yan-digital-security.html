STAYING SAFE ONLINE
7 Rules to Help You Avoid Online Scams

In the first six months of 2026 alone, Canadians reported approximately $351 million in fraud losses to the Canadian Anti-Fraud Centre. Perhaps more concerning, the CAFC estimates that only 5–10% of fraud is ever reported.

Investment fraud accounted for the largest reported losses, while spear phishing and bank-investigator scams were also significant. With this in mind, here are 7 rules to remember to keep you and your loved ones safe from harm.

1. Urgency is a reason to slow down, not speed up.

Scammers deliberately create pressure. They’ll use serious situations to make you feel that you need to act immediately:

  • Your account has been compromised.
  • Your grandchild is in trouble.
  • CRA needs payment.
  • Someone has accessed your credit card.

If someone is unexpectedly asking you for money, information or immediate action, stop.

Rule 1: The more urgent the request feels, the more important it is to verify it independently.

2. Don’t trust a phone number, email address or caller ID alone.

Caller ID can be spoofed. Emails and text messages can look remarkably legitimate. Fraudsters may even know personal information about you.

Rule 2: If something feels suspicious, hang up and contact the organization yourself using contact information you already trust, such as the number on the back of your bank card. Never click or use the phone number or link provided in the suspicious message.

3. Your bank will never need your help to “investigate” fraud.

No legitimate bank or police investigation requires you to move money to a “safe account,” send money to catch a criminal, give someone remote access to your computer, hand over your banking information, or disclose your password or verification code.

Rule 3: If someone asks you to transfer funds, disclose a password, banking details or verification code, or provide remote access to your computer, stop communicating with them and contact your financial institution directly.

4. A familiar voice may no longer be proof of identity.

Artificial intelligence can now be used to imitate the voice of a child, grandchild or another person you trust.

Rule 4: Agree on a family verification word or question that a stranger couldn’t easily discover online. If someone you love calls unexpectedly asking for money, hang up and call them yourself using the number you already have.

5. Turn on multi-factor authentication.

Use multi-factor authentication wherever it’s available, particularly for your email, banking and investment accounts, Apple or Google account, social media and cloud storage. Even if someone obtains your password, MFA provides another barrier between them and your information.

Rule 5: If an account offers multi-factor authentication, use it. Prioritize the accounts that provide access to your money, email and personal information.

6. Stop reusing passwords.

Use a different password for every account. Canada’s Get Cyber Safe recommends passphrases of at least four random words and 15 characters where possible. If remembering dozens of passwords sounds impossible, a password manager can help.

If you only change three passwords this month, start with your email, your primary financial account and your Apple or Google account, and make sure none of them are the same. Your email is particularly important because access to it can become a gateway to resetting passwords elsewhere.

Rule 6: Every important account should have its own password, especially your email account. If remembering them all is the problem, use a reputable password manager rather than recycling the same password.

7. When in doubt, call me.

If you receive a financial request that doesn’t feel right, particularly one involving an investment, transfer, or your IG accounts, I want to hear from you before you act.

You will never bother me by double-checking. I would much rather have a two-minute conversation about something legitimate than have you act on something that isn’t.

Rule 7: If something involving your finances doesn’t feel right, don’t act until you’ve verified it. When it involves your investments, transfers, or IG accounts, call me. You will never bother me by double-checking.

 
BEFORE YOU SHOP ONLINE
Tidy Up Your Digital Security for the Holidays

The holidays are one of the busiest times of year for online shopping, and scammers know it. A few minutes now can make your accounts much harder to break into before the gift lists start. Here’s a quick tune-up:

  1. Update your devices and apps. Install updates on your phone, tablet and computer, and turn on automatic updates so security fixes arrive as soon as they’re released.
  2. Check your account recovery details. Make sure the phone number and backup email on your email, banking and Apple or Google accounts are current, so you can get back in quickly if something goes wrong.
  3. Turn on purchase alerts. Most banks and credit card providers can text or email you when a purchase is made, so you’ll spot anything unusual right away.
  4. Use a credit card for online purchases. Credit cards generally offer stronger fraud protection than debit cards and keep your bank account one step removed from the purchase.
  5. Shop with retailers you know. Type the website address yourself rather than clicking on ads or links, and be wary of social media deals that seem too good to be true.
  6. Be cautious of delivery texts. Fake “missed delivery” and customs-fee messages are common this time of year. Check your order directly on the retailer’s or carrier’s website instead.
  7. Clear out old accounts and saved cards. Close online accounts you no longer use and remove saved payment details from sites you rarely shop with.
  8. Skip public Wi-Fi for banking and shopping. Use your mobile data or a trusted home network instead.
  9. Review your IG Online Access. Make sure you’re using a strong, unique password and that two-step verification is turned on.
