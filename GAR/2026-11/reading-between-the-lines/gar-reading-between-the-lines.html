"The pessimist complains about the wind; the optimist expects it to change; the realist adjusts the sails."
~William Arthur Ward

This past week the S&P 500 Index fell into correction territory with a drop of 11.6% from the high reached on October 6th. We often say that corrections are a normal part of a healthy and functioning market. They test investors’ resolve; force a redoubling of efforts with respect to price discovery and occasionally squeeze out the speculators that buy on hype and hope with little regard for the fundamentals.

Corrections are also, often uncomfortable. In addition to the S&P 500 Index’s pull-back, the NASDAQ Composite Index has given back all of its gains for 2026 following a deeper retreat of 18% from its October 7th high. Few markets were spared the gravitational pull of the NASDAQ and S&P 500 Indices. The S&P/TSX Index has also entered correction territory with a drop of -10.1% from its prior high reached in August.

The first thing we do as investment strategists when we experience a correction like this is ask: is anything broken with the economy? If the answer is no, then the volatility is characteristic of the many corrections of the past that typically occur on average every 18 months or so. What makes this volatility so interesting is that this is one we haven’t seen for a while. By that, I don’t mean that the volatility is unusual, but rather the trigger isn’t quite as obvious as the volatility of recent years.

For example, the near-correction we saw in the S&P 500 back in March, when it fell by 9.1%, we would argue was triggered by the U.S. initiating the war with Iran and surge in oil prices. Prior to that, the correction in the first quarter of 2025 was brought about by the announcement of global tariffs by the Trump administration. Both were shocks from outside the economy. To find a "normal" correction, we have to go back to the fall of 2023, when the 10-year yield climbed to 5% and the S&P 500 fell 10.2% between July and October.

This correction has echoes of that one in 2023: doubts about how quickly the heavy spending on artificial intelligence will pay off, a Fed expected to raise rates again in December and a 10-year Treasury yield back above 5%.

Today looks very similar to that environment. It also resembles the fourth quarter of 2018, when the Fed was raising rates, the U.S. 10-year yield had climbed to a multi-year high and stocks went through a valuation correction of almost 20%. In both cases, the economy kept growing, and the S&P 500 was more than 35% higher a year after its low.

So, is anything broken? We can’t find any reason to think so. A correction turns into a bear market in the face of economic stress. In other words, we would become much more cautious if we were seeing signs of an oncoming recession. From a historical context, these indicators would include: an inverted yield curve (not present), decelerating manufacturing activity (not present), rising unemployment (not present), tight financial conditions (not present) and others.

U.S. GDP grew at an annualized pace of 3.4% after inflation in the third quarter.

S&P 500 earnings rose 31.9% from a year earlier in the third quarter. All 11 sectors grew, 84% of companies beat estimates, and analysts expect growth of about 16% in 2027.

Bond markets, often the first to flag trouble, remain calm. The spread on U.S. high-yield bonds was 347 bps on Nov. 18, about where it stood at the end of October.

Brent crude has eased to about US$92 a barrel, from about US$105 at the start of November.

The S&P 500 traded at 16.2 times next year’s expected earnings, below its 5- and 10-year averages.

In other words, we do not see the typical signs of economic stress that bring about a bear market.

With that being the case, we believe the much higher likelihood is that this is a correction much like those of the past 75 years.

For context, the average correction since 1946 results in a drop in the S&P 500 Index by 14%, lasts 93 days, recovers to the prior high in 90 days with an average gain of 25% 1-year from the bottom.

With the probabilities leaning towards this being a correction characteristic of those in the past we turn our attention to the indicators that suggest we are at or near a bottom. Here is what we have seen:

The VIX , often referred to as the “fear gage” has broken above 30 (and even reached 40 intra-day). That level tends to correlate with a market bottom. When the VIX has broken above 30 in the past, the S&P 500 has been positive one year later 90% of the time, with an average return of 22%. Other indicators that suggest we may have reached peak capitulation include:

The put/call ratio broke above 1.1. This suggests an above average buying of downside protection – often an indicator a bottom is forming.

As of Friday’s close 45% of S&P 500 companies have fallen by more than 20% from their 52 week high. Since 1995 when more than 40% of companies are down more than 20% the one-year forward return averages 17.6% and delivers a positive gain 85% of the time.

Following the 3.7% drop on Friday the two-day decline totaled 5.6%. That would make Friday the 52nd worst 2-day drop for the S&P 500 Index since 1950. Of the 51 times that saw a similar or steeper decline, the one-year forward return averaged 24% with a positive gain in 46 out of the 51 years.

After the 4% drop on Nov. 5, we noted that options markets were pricing a rotation out of AI stocks, with little sign of contagion across the market. In our view, this is more price discovery than an all-out panic: markets are repricing AI leaders, inflation and the path of interest rates, in an economy that keeps growing.

Based on the above, while we can only be certain of a market bottom well after the fact, our confidence is increasing.

One of our themes for 2027 is "Don't sweat the brake check," and we would classify this correction as one of those brake checks. Our proprietary leading indicator still points to strong earnings growth through the first half of 2027. The signposts we track point the same way: the yield curve spread is below our yellow-alert levels, oil prices look capped and unlikely to rise much from here, and broad credit spreads remain below our warning levels. Equity valuations are cheaper from just a few weeks ago and much improved from a year ago.

Corrections are uncomfortable, and they are normal. And while we may not have reached bottom yet, there are enough indicators that we follow to suggest this correction is creating opportunities for long-term investors. Lastly, diversified portfolios, built for more than one version of 2027, are designed for weeks like this one.
