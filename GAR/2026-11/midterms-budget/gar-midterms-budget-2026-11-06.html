2026 Federal Budget highlights

The federal government presented its 2026 budget on November 5. Here are five proposed measures that could affect you:

One tax deadline for everyone. Starting with the 2027 tax year, individuals would file their return and pay any balance owing by May 15. Most people would get 15 more days, while self-employed individuals would file a month earlier than today’s June 15 deadline.

Fewer tax instalments. Starting in 2028, the instalment threshold would rise to $7,500 ($4,500 for Quebec residents) and be indexed to inflation. Individuals whose main income comes from a business could instead make a single instalment by December 31.

An “RRSP for life.” The government plans to combine RRSPs and RRIFs, so you would no longer have to convert your RRSP to a RRIF at age 71. Minimum withdrawals would still apply after 71. This is still subject to consultation, and no start date has been set.

Less foreign property reporting. The threshold for filing Form T1135 would double from $100,000 to $200,000 of specified foreign property, which is expected to remove the filing requirement for about one-third of taxpayers.

A new capital gains exemption for business owners. The Canada Growth Incentive would exempt up to $15 million in capital gains per individual on qualifying newly issued shares of Canadian-controlled private corporations held for at least 36 months. Some sectors are excluded, and claiming it could limit use of the Lifetime Capital Gains Exemption.

These are proposals and could change before they become law. If you’d like to talk about how any of them could affect your plan, we’re here to help.

After The Midterms: A rally, a reversal, and the bigger picture.

If you’ve been watching the headlines since the U.S. midterm election results, you may be wondering what this back-and-forth means for your investments. Here’s our perspective, drawing on the IG Investment Strategy Team’s view.

Short-term swings are normal after an election

Quick reversals like this are common in the days after a major event. Investors are working through a lot at once: what a new Congress could mean for policy, and the same questions about interest rates, inflation and earnings that were there before the vote. Prices can swing in both directions while that settles. A single day, up or down, tells us very little about where markets go from here.

What history tells us

Looking past the day-to-day, the longer-term pattern has been encouraging. Since 1950, the S&P 500 has been higher 12 months after every one of the 19 midterm elections, with an average gain of 15.1%. In the three midterms where the president’s party lost both chambers, as happened this week (1954, 1994 and 2006), the S&P 500 gained an average of 22.3% over the following year.

The calendar has also been friendly. Since 1950, November has been the S&P 500’s best month, with an average gain of 1.9%. In midterm years, that rises to 2.7%, with gains in 15 of 19 Novembers.

What this means for you

Your plan hasn’t changed. Elections and the market reactions that follow move headlines much more than they move long-term returns. Your portfolio is built around your goals, not around one week of trading.

Expect some noise. A new Congress brings policy debates, veto threats and shifts between market sectors. Days like Thursday are part of that, and they aren’t a reason to change course.

If you’ve been waiting on the sidelines, investing gradually over time can help ease concerns about timing, especially when markets are choppy. We’re happy to talk through what that could look like for you.

Diversification does the heavy lifting. A portfolio spread across regions and types of investments doesn’t depend on Washington getting along, or on any single day in the market.

History is a helpful guide, but it doesn’t guarantee future results. Nineteen elections is a small sample, and markets still face real risks, from interest rates to oil prices. Still, the takeaway is the same: short-term reactions come and go, and historically, patience through periods like this has been rewarded.
